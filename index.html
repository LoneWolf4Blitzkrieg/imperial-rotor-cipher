How it works

The X-10 is a digital model of an electromechanical rotor machine in the Enigma family. Every key press turns rotor 1. When a rotor reaches its turnover notch, a pawl pushes the next rotor along, and the notched rotor with it: Enigma’s double step. The signal runs through all ten rotors, is turned back by the reflector, and returns through the rotors to print one symbol.

Because the reflector sends the signal back along the same rotors, the machine undoes itself: set the same key and type the ciphertext to read the message. It also means no symbol ever enciphers to itself.

How strong is it?

The key is the ten window settings. Each has 94 positions, giving 9410 ≈ 5.4 × 1019 settings. The double step makes some settings act exactly like others, which leaves about 265.4 different keys, above 64 bits. Nine rotors would give only 259.

That counts keys; it is not the work needed to break a message. Rotor 2 turns once every 94 symbols and rotor 3 roughly once every 8,800, so in a short message the slow rotors usually stand still or turn only once or twice. Between those moments they act as one fixed scrambler, and someone who knows or guesses part of a message has only about 8,800 settings to try. Enigma fell to the same kind of weakness. Use the X-10 for learning, puzzles and fun, not for real secrets.

Send ciphertext as a file, an email or a code block: chat apps may treat *, _, ~ and ` as formatting and drop them.

Wirings, rules and self-test
  • The alphabet is the 94 visible keyboard characters, ! to ~. Spaces and line breaks are sent as ~, and ~ reads back as a space, so a typed ~ also comes back as a space. Other characters, such as tabs, accented letters and emoji, are refused.
  • The rotor wirings, turnover notches and reflector are fixed and published. They come from the public seed IMPERIAL-ROTOR-CIPHER/parts/v1, and the script in the project’s tools folder rebuilds them. Rotor 10’s notch has no effect, since no pawl sits beyond it.
  • Ciphertext is printed in groups of five. Spacing and line breaks are ignored when deciphering. One wrong symbol spoils only that symbol, but a missing or extra symbol garbles everything after it. Curly quotes, dashes and the ellipsis are changed to their plain keyboard forms.
